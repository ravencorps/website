Skills guide · Staying safe outdoors

How to stay warm outdoors and spot hypothermia

A winter walk in Britain is rarely dangerous, but cold, wind and rain together can catch anyone out. Staying warm is mostly about what you wear and how you look after each other.

The short answerDress in layers you can add and take off, avoid cotton, keep a dry spare layer, wear a hat and gloves, and eat and drink as you go. If someone starts shivering hard, fumbling, stumbling or talking nonsense, they may have hypothermia: get them out of the wind, swap wet clothes for dry, insulate them from the ground, give a warm sweet drink if they're fully awake, and call 999.

How to stay warm

  1. Wear three layers

    A base layer against your skin that moves sweat away (synthetic or merino wool), a fleece for warmth, and a windproof, waterproof jacket on top. Leave cotton at home: it holds moisture and leaves you damp, then cold.

  2. Cover your head and hands

    Wear a hat and gloves, and carry a spare pair of gloves. Work gloves are ideal for handling wood and keep your hands dry.

  3. Stay dry

    Take a layer off before you start to sweat, and put your waterproof on before you get wet, not after. Carry a dry spare layer in a bag.

  4. Watch the wind

    Wind and wet together take heat away fastest. Stop for breaks out of the wind, and be more careful when it's wet, windy and cold all at once.

  5. Eat and drink

    Food is fuel for keeping warm. Eat before you set off and snack as you go. A flask of something hot and sweet helps.

  6. Watch each other

    Go at the pace of the slowest person and check on anyone who has gone quiet. People with hypothermia often don't notice it themselves.

How to spot hypothermia

Hypothermia is when your body temperature drops below 35°C. It creeps up, and the person it's happening to is often the last to notice.

  • Early signs: shivering, feeling cold, fumbling with zips and laces, poor decisions, getting irritable or quiet. Outdoor instructors call them the umbles: grumbling, mumbling, stumbling and fumbling.
  • Getting worse: pale, cold skin; lips that look blue or grey (on darker skin, check the palms and the soles of the feet); slurred speech; slow breathing; tiredness and confusion.
  • Serious: the shivering stops. That doesn't mean they're warming up; it means they're getting colder.

What to do

The NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if you think someone has hypothermia. Don't let them drive. While you wait:

  1. Get them out of the wind and rain, into shelter or a group shelter.
  2. Take off wet clothes and put on dry layers, or wrap them in a sleeping bag or blanket. Keep their head covered.
  3. Get them off the cold ground: sit them on a rucksack, a mat or spare clothes.
  4. If they're fully awake, give them a warm, non-alcoholic drink and something sugary to eat.
  5. Keep talking to them, and stay with them until help arrives.
Don't: use a hot bath, a hot water bottle, a heat lamp or a fire to warm them, rub their arms and legs, or give them alcohol. Rewarm them slowly and handle them gently.

A word about the hipflask

Every Raven Corps meetup ends at The Roost, and some of us bring a hipflask. But alcohol doesn't warm you up: it sends blood to your skin, which feels warm while your core cools, and it dulls your sense of the cold. So it comes at the very end, in dry layers, and anyone who's cold gets the hot drink instead.

Questions

What body temperature counts as hypothermia?

Below 35°C. Normal body temperature is around 37°C, so it doesn't take a big drop. Severe hypothermia can kill if it isn't spotted and treated quickly.

Does a hipflask keep you warm?

No. Alcohol makes you feel warmer by sending blood to your skin, but that draws heat away from your core, and it dulls your sense of the cold. Keep the hipflask for the end of the day, in dry layers, and give anyone who's cold a hot, sweet, non-alcoholic drink.

Can you get hypothermia when it isn't freezing?

Yes. Being wet and in the wind takes heat away far faster than still, dry air, so people can get dangerously cold on a wet, windy day well above freezing.

Should I warm someone up by a fire or with a hot water bottle?

No. Rewarm them slowly: shelter, dry clothes, insulation from the ground and a warm drink. The NHS says not to use a hot bath, a hot water bottle or a heat lamp, and not to rub their arms and legs.

What should I carry on a winter walk?

A spare warm layer, a hat and gloves, food and a hot drink, a charged phone, a torch, and for a group, a group shelter or survival bag to get someone out of the wind.

This is general information, not medical advice. In an emergency, call 999.
