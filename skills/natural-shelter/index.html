How to build a shelter from natural materials

Building a shelter from what the woods give you teaches you how shelter really works. It's also one of the best things to do with a group.

The short answerPick a safe spot with no dead branches overhead and out of any hollow. Prop a strong ridge pole on a tree fork, lean sticks against it, weave twigs across them, then pile on dead leaves until the layer is about an arm's length thick. Use only fallen wood, ask the landowner first, and take it down when you leave.

Step by step

  1. Get permission

    Building a shelter on someone's land needs their agreement, whether it's Forestry England, the National Trust, a council or a farmer. Many landowners are happy for small groups to build shelters if you ask first.

  2. Choose a safe spot

    Look up before anything else: no dead branches hanging above you, and no dead trees nearby. Avoid hollows and dry stream beds, where cold air and water collect. Use a bank or a fallen tree as a windbreak.

  3. Set the ridge pole

    Find a strong fallen branch a bit longer than you are tall. Rest one end in the fork of a tree, or on two sticks tied into an A-frame, at about waist height. The other end rests on the ground for a debris shelter, or on a second support for a lean-to.

  4. Add the ribs

    Lean sticks against the ridge pole, close together, on one side for a lean-to or both sides for a debris shelter. Keep the inside just big enough to lie in: a small space is a warm space.

  5. Weave a lattice

    Lay thinner, twiggy branches across the ribs. They stop the leaves falling through.

  6. Pile on the leaves

    Heap dead leaves, bracken and other dry debris over the frame, starting at the bottom and working up, so rain runs off. Make it far thicker than looks sensible: roughly an arm's length deep to keep out rain and cold.

  7. Insulate the floor

    You lose most heat into the ground, so fill the inside with a thick bed of dry leaves to lie on.

  8. Test it and take it down

    Pour a cup of water over the top and see if it comes through. When you're done, take the shelter apart and scatter the wood and leaves, so the spot looks as you found it.

Lean-to or debris shelter?

A lean-to has a ridge pole between two supports and a roof on one side only. It's quick to build and open at the front, which is good with a fire in front of it, where a fire is allowed. A debris shelter has ribs on both sides and one closed end, like a long tent of leaves. It takes longer but it's much warmer.

Stay safe: never build under dead or hung-up branches, don't climb on a shelter, carry poles upright rather than across your body, and wear gloves for splinters.

Questions

Is it legal to build a shelter in the woods in the UK?

Only with the landowner's permission. In England and Wales there's no general right to build shelters or camp on other people's land, even in woods that are open to walkers. Scotland's access rules are different. Ask first: many landowners say yes to small groups who leave no trace.

How long does a debris shelter take to build?

Allow a few hours for one you could sleep in, and longer in a group's first go. Start well before dark.

Can I cut branches to build it?

No. Use only fallen, dead wood. Cutting or damaging living trees needs the landowner's permission and harms the woodland.

Will a debris shelter keep me warm?

A well-built one, with a thick roof and a thick leaf bed, keeps you out of the wind and rain and slows heat loss a lot. It works with your clothing, not instead of it.
