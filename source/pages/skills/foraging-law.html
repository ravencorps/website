What you can and can't forage in the UK

Blackberries in September, wild garlic in spring, hazelnuts in autumn. Foraging is one of the best ways to get to know the countryside, as long as you stay legal and safe.

The short answerIn England and Wales, picking wild fruit, flowers, leaves and fungi for your own use isn't theft, as long as you're somewhere you're allowed to be. You can't uproot any wild plant without the landowner's permission, pick protected plants, or pick to sell without permission. Nature reserves and local byelaws can add their own bans. And only eat what you're certain of.

What the law says

Picking for yourself is allowed

Under the Theft Act 1968, picking mushrooms and other fungi, or the flowers, fruit and foliage of plants growing wild, isn't theft, even on someone else's land. That protection doesn't apply if you're picking for sale, for reward or for any other commercial purpose.

You still need to be allowed there

The Theft Act doesn't give you a right to walk anywhere. Stick to public rights of way, open access land and places such as Forestry England woods where visitors are welcome.

Don't uproot anything

Under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, it's an offence to uproot any wild plant without permission from the landowner or occupier. Digging up wild garlic bulbs or roots counts.

Leave protected plants alone

Some rare plants, listed in Schedule 8 of the same Act, are protected completely: you can't pick, uproot or sell them at all.

Watch for special sites and byelaws

On Sites of Special Scientific Interest and nature reserves, damaging the features that make the site special is an offence, and many reserves ban picking altogether. Landowners such as councils and the National Trust can also have byelaws. Check the signs, and if in doubt, leave it.

The safety rules

  • Be 100% certain. Check every feature against more than one reliable guide. If in doubt, leave it out.
  • Learn the deadly ones first. Hemlock and hemlock water dropwort (white, umbrella-shaped flowers), death cap mushrooms, and the plants that grow among wild garlic.
  • Pick in clean places, away from busy roads, sprayed fields and old industrial land, and higher up than dogs can reach.
  • Wash everything, and cook what needs cooking: elderberries, for example, must be cooked.
  • Try a little first, and keep a sample in case you feel unwell.
  • Take a small share, and leave plenty for wildlife and for the plant to grow back.
If someone eats something poisonous: call 999 if they're seriously unwell, or NHS 111 if you're not sure. Take a sample of what they ate.
